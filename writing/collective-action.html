What is the collective action problem?

There is a gap between what is good for each person individually and what is good for everyone together. This gap is not a failure of intelligence or character. It is a structural feature of many situations, and understanding it is one of the more important things one can do to understand how the world works.

The collective action problem appears wherever a group could achieve something by coordinating — but where each individual, reasoning from their own position, finds it rational not to contribute. The result is that the thing never gets built, the resource gets destroyed, the standard never gets adopted, the security protocol never gets updated.

What makes this worth writing about is how the same underlying structure recurs across fields that rarely talk to each other: a political scientist studying state formation, an economist modelling carbon emissions, a game theorist analysing arms races, and a security engineer thinking about why TLS implementations drift from their specifications — are all, in a precise sense, looking at the same problem. These notes try to hold all of that together.

Political science: the problem of cooperation

Mancur Olson's The Logic of Collective Action (1965) is the uncomfortable starting point. Olson's argument was that large groups, even when they share a common interest, tend not to act on it — not because members are ignorant of that interest, but precisely because each member knows that if the group succeeds, they will benefit whether or not they contributed. The incentive to free-ride is not a character flaw; it is built into the structure.

This was a direct challenge to the pluralist assumption that interest groups naturally form and act on shared interests. Olson showed that small groups are more effective than large ones — not despite having fewer members, but because of it. In a small group, each member's contribution visibly matters. Defection is noticeable. The logic inverts at scale.

More to come: selective incentives, the state as enforcement mechanism, Elinor Ostrom's challenge to Olson, and what this means for democratic governance.

Game theory: prisoners and stags

Coming. The prisoner's dilemma is the textbook case, but the stag hunt is more interesting — it asks not whether to defect, but whether to trust. The difference matters. Also: iterated games, reputation, and why the shadow of the future changes everything.

Information security: protocols as collective action

Coming. Security protocols require every participant to follow a specification — but implementations drift. No single implementer has an incentive to maintain strict conformance if others don't. The formal verification problem is, in one reading, a collective action problem. And the solution — automated model extraction — changes the incentive structure.

Ostrom's escape: governing the commons

Coming. Elinor Ostrom's Nobel-winning work showed that the tragedy of the commons is not inevitable — that communities develop sophisticated governance structures to manage shared resources without privatisation or state control. The conditions under which this works, and why they are so often absent at scale, are worth understanding carefully.
